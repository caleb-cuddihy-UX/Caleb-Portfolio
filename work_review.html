Re:View

A reflective reading companion that observes how you move through a page — scroll rhythm, hesitation, re-reads — and surfaces those patterns back to you after the session. No interruptions while you read. No judgements, ever. The meaning is always yours to decide.

The problem

Most reading tools are built around consumption — they track pages, highlight text, and measure time. What they don't do is help readers understand how they engage with what they read. Why did you slow down on that chapter? Why did you return to that passage three times? Was it difficulty, interest, distraction — or something else?

Re:View is built on the observation that reading behaviour is rich with signal, and that surfacing that signal — without interpreting it — gives readers something genuinely useful: a mirror, not a verdict.

The design challenge: How do we build a companion that observes reading behaviour accurately, surfaces it honestly, and hands meaning back to the reader — without ever feeling like surveillance?

Understanding the users Discover

Re:View was my MSc dissertation. I combined the Double Diamond with design thinking: the pairing encouraged freedom and breadth in research and ideation early on, then brought the work back into focus as the project moved into each new phase. The aim was that every decision would be backed by evidence and user insight, with alternatives considered.

Insight 01

Readers notice lost focus only after it has happened

Readers have limited awareness of their own reading behaviour. They recognise lost focus afterwards, skip words without noticing, and become less self-aware the longer a reading session runs.

Design response: show reading behaviour back to the reader after the session, when they are in a position to make sense of it.

Insight 02

AI already helps people read, but not to understand how they read

Readers use AI to summarise, simplify and check their understanding. A review of the tools on the market showed the same pattern: products optimise for speed and efficiency, and very few support reflection or self-awareness.

Design response: position Re:View in that gap, as reflective, autonomy-preserving support rather than another summariser.

Insight 03

Trust depends on how the AI behaves, not whether it is there

Interviews surfaced unease about being observed, judged, replaced or made dependent. Trust was shaped less by the presence of AI than by how respectfully it communicates, explains its observations and leaves the reader in control. In readers’ words: “Please don’t interrupt me while I’m reading” and “I don’t want AI telling me what to do.”

Design response: observation is opt-in and visible, nothing interrupts reading, and feedback is gentle and optional.

Insight 04

Gaze is not comprehension

An exploratory eye-tracking study tested whether reading behaviour could be observed at all. It could: the approach proved viable. It also showed its limit. One participant read attentively by every gaze measure, yet described feeling less absorbed on the later pages.

Design response: eye tracking was left out of the final design after user feedback, but the lesson stayed. Behavioural signals are prompts for reflection, never conclusions, and the reader’s account outranks the system’s.

Defining the focus Define

Synthesis turned the research into six themes, each with a takeaway the design had to honour. A separate project working from a similar brief explored a proactive system that makes changes in real time. Re:View deliberately took the other path: reflective support that deepens a reader’s understanding of their own behaviour while keeping them in control.

Insight 05

Six principles for trustworthy reflective AI

The takeaways became six design principles: permission before observation, evidence before conclusions, reflection over instruction, transparency builds trust, the user owns interpretation, and learning through collaboration.

Design response: they became the test every screen and every line of copy had to pass.

Insight 06

The AI supports interpretation; the reader keeps authority

A conversation model mapped the exchange between reader and system across six stages, from establishing trust and granting permission through to collaborative interpretation and learning over time. For each stage it sets out what the reader does, what the system does and the shared understanding between them.

Design response: the onboarding, the session flow and the “Reflect together” tool follow these stages in order.

Four principles a reader notices most

Permission, always

Observation begins only when the user starts a session, and ends the moment they finish. Nothing is tracked outside that window. The system is transparent about when it is and isn't watching — and the onboarding makes this the first thing a new user learns.

No interruptions while reading

Nothing appears on screen during a reading session. The companion is silent. Reflection happens afterwards, on the reader's terms.

Confidence, not certainty

Every observation the system surfaces carries a confidence score and the evidence behind it. When the system isn't sure, it says so explicitly. It will never present an uncertain finding as fact.

The reader always outranks the system

When a reader disagrees with an observation, their context matters more. Saying "I was just tired" is a valid and complete correction — the system adjusts its model accordingly.

Designing the solution Develop

The low-fi prototype was hand-drawn and tested as a hard copy. From mid-fi onwards I designed primarily in Figma. Lovable was used for fast iteration, visualising different styles to gauge how well they suited the project’s aims without spending excess time and resource. A more minimal approach, with elements of Claude, later supported rapid iteration and experimentation with edits. The final designs were built and wired in Figma.

Onboarding

A short onboarding flow establishes the trust model before the reader ever opens a book. Permission, no interruptions, reader interpretation, and the system’s learning loop — each introduced clearly and without jargon.

Step 1

Welcome

The first screen makes the promise the rest of the product keeps: read the way you like, and reflect afterwards, together.

Step 2

How it works

Explains what Re:View observes, and just as clearly what it does not. It watches how you move through a page, not what the words mean.

Step 3

After reading

Sets the expectation for what comes back: each session leaves a record of how you read, not what you read.

Step 4

Introduces the learning loop. When the reader agrees with or challenges an observation, the system learns from it.

The core app

Home

Dashboard

The home screen shows reading history at a glance — recent sessions, total time, moments noticed — and gives direct access to all reflective tools. The “Start reading” button is prominent; the observation reminder is calm and persistent at the bottom of the sidebar.

Reading

Reading session

The reading screen is intentionally minimal. A floating timer widget is the only indication the system is active, and observation stays off until the reader starts.

Reading

Session complete

On session end, a modal confirms that observation has ended and permission has expired, then offers two routes: back to the dashboard, or straight into reflection.

Reflection tools

Four tools give readers different ways into their session data.

Tool 1

Reflection summary

The session as a whole, written as a description, not an assessment. Nothing here is a judgement.

Tool 2

Behaviour timeline

Individual moments from the session, each one with its evidence attached.

Tool 3

Insight cards

What Re:View observed, with the system’s confidence shown on every card and a way to say “that doesn’t seem right”.

Tool 4

Reflect together

A free-text reflection journal, saved privately. Write as much or as little as you like.

Across sessions

Reading journey

Surfaces patterns across sessions — time spent, reading rhythm and longer trends — framed always as patterns rather than performance. There is no target to hit and nothing to keep up with.

Key design decisions

Language as the primary design material

The writing throughout Re:View is as considered as the interface. "A description, not an assessment." "Nothing here is a judgement, just data." "The meaning is always yours to decide." These aren't taglines — they're the product's promise, repeated consistently across every screen where the reader might feel observed or evaluated.

Confidence scores over clean answers

The decision to show confidence scores on every observation was the most contentious in the project. A cleaner interface would hide the uncertainty. But surfacing it — "73% confident, based on 6 sessions" — is more honest and ultimately more useful. Readers who understand what the system is and isn't sure about can engage with its findings more intelligently.

The reflection loop as the product's core

Most analytics tools surface data and stop. Re:View's central hypothesis is that the most valuable moment is after the data is shown — when the reader decides what it means. The "Reflect together" tool and the system's ability to update its model based on reader corrections are the features that make Re:View a companion rather than a tracker.

Testing and iterating Deliver · Test

I tested three prototypes, from paper sketches through mid-fidelity to the hi-fi design, with five participants in each round. What they found changed the product.

The confidence score: from misread by everyone to understood by everyone

Low-fi
5 of 5

participants misunderstood the confidence score, reading it as their reading ability, their comprehension or their own confidence. The AI’s uncertainty was being mistaken for an assessment of the reader.

Mid-fi
4 of 5

still misunderstood it after I added supporting explanations. “I don’t understand if it’s from my side or… the tool’s side.” The wording had changed, but the underlying mental model was still wrong.

Hi-fi
5 of 5

interpreted the score correctly, after a redesign that labelled it as the system’s confidence and separated evidence from interpretation. Three of them identified some minor lingering ambiguity about what the percentage represented. Severity fell from high to medium.

Improved, with minor ambiguity remaining. To address it, the final wording became “System confidence in this interpretation”, with an explanation of what contributes to the score. It is the first thing I would test again.

What else testing changed

  • “An awful lot of things” in the navigation. The menu mixed destinations with stages of the reading workflow. I simplified it and took workflow states out of the permanent navigation.
  • Session controls were expected while reading. Participants looked for pause, stop and complete alongside the text. A compact observation widget now keeps status and controls in the reading context.
  • “I didn’t really get it.” A participant took a pause to mean they had not understood. Observable evidence is now separated from the AI’s interpretation, so an observation is never presented as fact about the reader.
  • “I see a little thing that says active, but I don’t know what it means.” The calm interface had become too subtle. Active, pause and end states are clearer, buttons are stronger and contrast is improved, without losing the calm styling.

Reflection

The hardest design problem in Re:View wasn't technical — it was trust. Building a product that observes people's behaviour and reflects it back to them sits in genuinely uncomfortable territory. Every decision about language, transparency, permission and framing was made with that discomfort in mind.

If I were to continue the project, the most important next step would be research with readers who have complicated relationships with their own reading — people with dyslexia, ADHD, or anxiety around academic performance — to understand whether the current framing genuinely neutralises the evaluative feeling, or just moves it.

I would also keep refining the confidence score. It improved in every round of testing, and by the final round all five participants interpreted it correctly, though three still identified some minor ambiguity. A tool built on honesty about uncertainty has to get that right.
